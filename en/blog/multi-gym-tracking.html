Training at Multiple Gyms? How to Keep Weight Logs Separate

September 14, 2026 · Updated September 21, 2026

Maybe you split your time between a home setup and a commercial gym. Maybe you travel for work and end up at a different gym every other week. Whatever the reason, plenty of lifters train in more than one place — and it usually leads to the same confusing moment: you load up the weight that felt fine last time, and suddenly it's noticeably harder (or suspiciously easy).

Why the "same weight" doesn't feel the same

Several things are stacking up here. First, equipment varies more than you'd think. Two machines both labeled "leg press" can have different seat angles, different ranges of motion, and different plate increments. Cable machines add friction and pulley ratios into the mix, so the number on the stack doesn't always match the resistance you actually feel.

Even free weights aren't immune — bench height, the actual weight of a "20kg" barbell (there's always some tolerance), and even how firm the floor is can add up. On top of all that, your own day-to-day condition plays a role too, which makes it hard to tell whether a harder set at a new gym means you're actually weaker, or the environment is just different.

What happens if you lump the records together

If every gym's numbers feed into one combined log per exercise, these environmental differences start showing up as fake "progress" or "regression" on your chart. You might be improving steadily, but a session logged at a different gym drags the line down and makes it look like you're backsliding — which is discouraging even though nothing's actually wrong. The reverse is also a risk: a lighter machine somewhere else can trick you into loading too much weight back at your usual gym.

The fix: treat each location as its own log

The straightforward solution is to treat the same exercise name as a separate record whenever the location (gym or machine) is different. That way, each location's numbers only ever compete with themselves — which matters most when the load characteristics differ a lot, like home training versus a commercial gym.

  • Keep "leg press at Gym A" and "leg press at Gym B" as two independent records
  • Track "last time" and personal bests separately for each location
  • Let your menus (workout plans) stay location-agnostic — they just define which exercises to do

How to actually do this

If you're using a plain notebook or a simple notes app, appending the location to the exercise name ("Leg Press – Gym A") works, but it adds friction to every entry and makes it harder to search your history by exercise later.

If you're using a logging app, check whether it lets you register the same exercise separately per location. When it does, each set gets automatically filed under the right location, and looking back later shows "Leg Press at Gym A" and "Leg Press at Gym B" as two clean, independent trends.

How to pick your weights on a new gym day

At a gym or machine you haven't used before, treat last time's weight as a rough reference rather than a target. A sensible flow looks like this:

  1. Start with one lighter set on the first exercise, just to feel how the equipment behaves.
  2. Judge the next set by how hard that one felt. Anchoring on RPE (rate of perceived exertion) instead of the number on the stack makes you much less sensitive to equipment differences.
  3. Log that day's weight and reps as that location's record. Don't read it as "I got weaker than at my other gym" — it's simply the first reference point for this place.

From the second visit on, use that location's previous numbers as your baseline and go back to adding a little weight or a rep at a time, following progressive overload.

Keep the small machine settings with the log

Even on the same machine, seat height, backrest angle, pad position, and grip width change how heavy a set feels and what it works. If you move between gyms, just remembering those settings each time takes effort. When you keep the settings next to your log, you can start under the same conditions as last time by simply matching them, and your weight comparisons stay much cleaner.

Don't split things more than you need to

Separating by location doesn't mean tracking every single machine on its own. A few rules of thumb:

  • Split when the equipment is clearly different, like home training versus a commercial gym
  • Branches of the same chain with the same brand and model of machine can reasonably be grouped together
  • Use short, consistent location names such as "Home" and "Gym A", and tidy them up if they multiply

A realistic approach is to start by separating only the places you visit most, then manage individual exercises separately where the mismatch feels biggest.

Common questions

Do I need to split how I structure my training between home and the gym?

No. Ideas like a "chest day" or a "leg day" can stay the same everywhere. Only the weight records need to be separate — as long as you can tell which location a record came from, you can reuse the same kind of plan.

Is there a way to convert weights between gyms?

Because machine design and settings differ, there is no single formula that works. Rather than forcing a conversion, log your first few sessions at a new place as the baseline and track your progress from there.

What if I can't remember my last weight?

Keep that location's previous numbers easy to check. In a notebook, add the location to the exercise name; in an app, register the exercise per location so you can compare the last date and weight side by side.
