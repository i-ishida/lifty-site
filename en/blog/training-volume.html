What Is Training Volume? How to Calculate It and Beat Plateaus

September 9, 2026

There are stretches where your top weight barely budges, yet you're actually doing more work than ever. If weight is the only number you're tracking, you'll miss this — and might conclude you're stuck when you're not.

How to calculate volume

Training volume is calculated with a simple formula:

Volume = weight × reps × sets

For example, 80kg for 10 reps across 3 sets gives you a volume of 80 × 10 × 3 = 2,400kg. For pyramid or ascending-weight sets where weight and reps change set to set, calculate each set's "weight × reps" individually, then add them up (e.g., 80kg×10 + 85kg×8 + 90kg×6 = 800 + 680 + 540 = 2,020kg).

Why volume matters

One of the core principles behind muscle growth and strength gains is progressive overload — gradually increasing the demand you place on your body. Weight is just one lever for that; reps, sets, and frequency are others. Volume rolls all of these into a single number, so it can capture progress even when "the weight is the same but the reps went up," or "the weight went up but the sets went down."

This matters most during plateaus, when adding weight isn't realistic. Maintaining or increasing volume through reps or sets is often a viable path forward — and it's not unusual to feel "stuck" by weight alone while your volume chart tells a different, more encouraging story.

Ways to increase volume

  • Add weight — the most direct lever, but also the hardest on joints and form
  • Add reps — push out more reps at the same weight
  • Add sets — do more total sets of the exercise
  • Train more often — hit the same muscle group an extra time per week

Realistically, you'll mix and match these based on how you're feeling and what a given exercise allows, rather than leaning on just one.

A caution about comparing volume

Volume is most meaningful when compared within the same exercise over time. Adding up squat volume and bench press volume into one grand total doesn't tell you much, since the muscles and ranges of motion involved are completely different. Tracking "this week's squat volume vs. last week's" is the practical way to use it.

And volume is a means, not an end — chasing bigger numbers at the cost of form or recovery defeats the purpose. Treat it as a supporting signal for whether you're moving forward, and balance it against how your body actually feels.
